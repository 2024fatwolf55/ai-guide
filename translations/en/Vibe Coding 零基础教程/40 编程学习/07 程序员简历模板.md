# Programmer Resume Template

> In the AI era, write a programmer resume that makes interviewers’ eyes light up

Hello, I’m programmer Yupi.

Every job-hunting season, I share some career advice. Over the years, I’ve helped review thousands of resumes. Without exaggeration, the vast majority of students’ resumes can only be described as "painful to look at."

![](https://pic.yupi.icu/1/image-20260330153426705.png)

But more importantly, with the rise of AI, the way programmers write resumes also has to change!

In today’s hiring market, knowing AI is no longer a bonus point — it’s a **screening condition**. Just browse the JDs from major companies and you’ll see that many explicitly say: candidates familiar with large-model development are preferred, and those with AI coding experience are preferred. If your resume is still filled with nothing but CRUD projects and doesn’t contain even a single AI-related keyword, there’s a high chance you’ll be filtered out in the very first round.

And in the AI era, the boundaries of a programmer’s capabilities have expanded a lot. In the past, when we said "full stack," it might just mean frontend plus backend. But now? You need to know traditional backend development, AI application development, how to use AI coding tools to boost efficiency, and even product thinking, data analysis, monitoring, and operations... With AI’s help, many things can now be done faster than before. The key is knowing **which directions you should put your effort into**.

So in this article, I’m not just teaching you how to write a resume. I also spent quite a bit of time polishing a sample called **"The Perfect Programmer Resume for the AI Era"** for everyone to study and use as a reference. First I’ll show you the full resume, and then I’ll go through it section by section to explain: why write it this way? How is it different from a traditional resume? What exactly are employers looking for?

After reading this article, you’ll gain at least 3 things:

1. A clear learning direction: understand which technologies companies are really looking for, so you no longer blindly grind tutorials
2. More interview opportunities: keep your resume from falling behind in the AI era and increase the chance of being selected by interviewers
3. An employer’s perspective: learn to view resumes from the hiring side and achieve two-way matching between you and the company

## Resume Writing Guide for the AI Era



### Personal Information

First, look at the beginning of the resume:

![](https://pic.yupi.icu/1/image-20260330155917595.png)

The first thing to notice is how the job intention is written. In the past, people usually wrote something like "Java Backend Development" or "Frontend Development." But now I suggest adding a parenthetical note after it, for example: `Java Backend Development (with AI Agent full-stack development experience)`, to clearly highlight your AI-related experience.

Why? Because when HR screens resumes, they may only spend 10 seconds on one. It’s like scrolling short videos: if the first 3 seconds don’t grab attention, they swipe away. Since you really do have AI Agent development experience, you should tell them immediately in the most visible spot. That greatly increases your chances of being selected.

Then note this: **you must put your portfolio website and GitHub at the top of your resume!**

Many students’ resumes only list their name, phone number, and email, without even a GitHub link. In the past, not having a website was understandable, since building one took a lot of time. But now? With AI coding tools, you can make a pretty decent personal portfolio site in just one hour, at very low cost.

Out of 10 resumes I’ve seen, probably 8 have no online project address at all. If you do have one, interviewers can click in directly and see what you’ve built, instantly setting you apart. **Launching real projects online is the best way to increase credibility — bar none.** And with AI coding being so hot, building a portfolio site itself is proof of your initiative and ability.



### Education Background and Awards

![](https://pic.yupi.icu/1/image-20260330160113442.png)

There’s nothing especially new in this section, but there are 3 small tricks worth noting.

1) Education background and awards can be combined into one section

For most students, education only takes one line, and awards only take one or two lines. There’s no need to split them into two separate sections. Combining them is more compact, and the space you save can be used for work experience and project experience, which are the real core of the resume.

2) Should you write your academic ranking?

If you rank in the top 20%, write it confidently. Personally, I think strong grades are definitely a plus. But if the ranking doesn’t look very good, then don’t actively expose your weakness.

3) Carefully adjust the order of your awards

Put the awards with high recognition and strong relevance to the target job first. Unknown competitions can go later, or you can omit them altogether. Don’t pile every random award onto the page. For example, Yupi once won "6th place in the men’s division of No.1 Middle School’s Kun Dance Competition." If interviewers see a bunch of competitions they’ve never heard of, they’ll just think you’re padding the resume.



### Professional Skills

This section is where the entire resume changes the most, and it’s also the part I most want to talk about.



#### Development Technologies

First, look at the traditional development technology part:

![](https://pic.yupi.icu/1/image-20260330160457275.png)

Taking backend as an example, the first 5 items should all be familiar: Java, Spring Boot, MySQL, Redis, and Docker. These are the foundations of backend development. No matter how AI develops, these technologies are still the most widely used in companies, and they’ll very likely show up in interviews.

But I especially want you to pay attention to the last two items.

**The sixth item: monitoring and alerting systems**

You might think: isn’t that operations work?

In the past, yes. But now more and more companies expect backend developers to understand monitoring and be able to troubleshoot online issues. Especially in small and medium-sized companies, nobody is going to assign you a dedicated ops team. When something breaks, you solve it yourself.

So if your resume includes keywords like Prometheus, Grafana, and ARMS, interviewers will feel that you have a big-picture mindset — not just someone who writes code, throws it over the wall, and thinks "if it runs, it’s good enough."

**The seventh item: SEO and GEO**

I’m guessing many students have never even heard these two terms, and I rarely see them on resumes of people with less than 3 years of experience.

Simply put, SEO is search engine optimization — making your website rank higher on Baidu and Google. GEO is generative engine optimization — making your content easier for AI tools such as Perplexity, Doubao, and ChatGPT to quote and recommend to users.

Why should a backend developer understand this? Because now the cost of "writing code" is getting lower and lower. AI coding tools make implementing features much easier than before. So **the things before coding** and **the things after coding** are becoming more important.

Before coding, you need good ideas, sound solution design, and judgment about different technical choices. After coding, you need to launch the project, promote it, build monitoring, analyze data, and make the product truly run and continuously create value.



#### AI Application Development

Now look at the AI-related skills:

![](https://pic.yupi.icu/1/image-20260330161157605.png)

Don’t be fooled by how short these 4 items look. Every one of them was carefully designed and layered. Let me break them down for you.

**Level 1: AI development frameworks**

This is the core technical capability. For Java programmers, frameworks such as Spring AI and LangChain4j are the AI-era equivalent of Spring Boot — the main tools companies use to build AI applications. RAG, MCP, and Agents are also all things real companies are actually using. If you look at AI development job descriptions, these are exactly the keywords that appear most frequently.

**Level 2: AI application practice**

This item reflects your ability to truly put AI to use. Many people think that calling the API of a large model means they understand AI development, but what companies need is someone who can build effective RAG knowledge bases, optimize prompts so the model outputs more accurately, and design AI products that people actually want to use. These abilities directly determine whether what you build has real value.

**Level 3: AI coding tools**

More and more companies now hope programmers know how to use tools like Cursor, Claude Code, and GitHub Copilot, because people who use these tools effectively produce much more efficiently. But more importantly, you shouldn’t just know how to use AI tools — you should also be able to build tools, MCP Servers, and Agent Skills to extend them. Most people are just users of AI tools. But if you can also contribute new capabilities to those tools, that immediately separates you from people who "only know how to use AI." If you want to avoid unnecessary pitfalls, you can read the related articles in the tips-and-techniques section of this tutorial, which contains lots of practical AI coding tricks.

![](https://pic.yupi.icu/1/image-20260330162015019.png)

**Level 4: AI coding paradigms**

This is the methodology level. Vibe Coding is currently the most popular AI coding paradigm, SDD is a specification-driven development model, and Harness Engineering is a more systematic AI engineering practice. Once you write these on your resume, interviewers will understand that you’re not just following the AI trend casually — you’re seriously thinking about how to make AI coding more professional and controllable.

Some students may worry: if traditional company interviewers see these new AI technologies, will they think it’s too advanced? On the contrary, **if your resume still doesn’t include any AI-related skills this year, that’s what’s scary!** When recruiters see a resume full of traditional technologies and not a single AI-related keyword, their first reaction may be: is this person not interested in new technologies?

Of course, I’m not telling you to fabricate these skills. First go learn them, go practice them, and then naturally write them into your resume. For example, by following the latest AI coding and AI super-agent projects we developed at [Programming Navigation](https://codefather.cn/), in less than one month you can basically master most of the AI coding and AI application development skills companies need.

Also, don’t write too many professional skills. Make sure interviewers can still see your work experience or project experience on the first page of your resume. After all, what they care about most is what you’ve actually done. The skill list is just for quickly judging your technical direction and depth.



### Work Experience

![](https://pic.yupi.icu/1/image-20260330162926648.png)

At the beginning of the work experience section, I recommend using one line to clearly explain the product you were responsible for and its data scale. For example, include the product’s public URL, and list a metric like daily active users, PV, or registered users — just one representative scale metric is enough. The reason is simple: interviewers need to judge whether this product is real, and what level of product it is.

If confidentiality or other reasons make it inconvenient to provide the online product address, you can also provide a desensitized documentation link, containing result screenshots, user feedback, the modules you were responsible for, and similar content. These are all great ways to increase credibility.

When writing work experience, the most important thing is showing **what kinds of abilities you demonstrated on the job**. I’ve summarized 3 key dimensions:

1) Architecture design ability

What interviewers love to see most is that you’ve done system design from 0 to 1. For example, "Led the design and development of a unified payment center from 0 to 1" immediately shows that you didn’t just write CRUD inside a framework built by someone else — you designed system architecture yourself. Even if you haven’t done a big architecture project like that yet, it’s still okay. Any work where you made technical choices, designed solutions, or made trade-off decisions is worth writing down.

2) Technical foresight and implementation ability

If you’re applying for a development role, simply saying "researched AI technologies" is meaningless. Companies need to see that you combined new technologies with real business and **created value**. For example, "Integrated large-model capabilities into an existing business system, increasing the interception rate of non-compliant UGC content to 98%." One sentence explains the business scenario and the result.

3) Operations and problem-solving ability

Many students never mention monitoring or operations-related content in their work experience. But in companies, "write the code and then stop caring" is not acceptable. You need to take responsibility for your code all the way through, including monitoring after deployment, alerting, and troubleshooting. If your resume includes this kind of content, interviewers will think you’re reliable and able to take ownership independently.

To sum up: don’t just write what features you built. Show **what decisions you made, what technical solutions you used, and what results you achieved**. This same way of thinking also applies to internship experience.



### Project Experience

In the AI era, choosing project experience is a very strategic thing!

You can see that I included two projects: an AI super agent, and an AI hot-topic monitoring tool. Both projects contain AI-related keywords, but their emphases are completely different.

AI Super Agent: focuses on the combination of traditional Java backend technology and AI application development, showing new AI-direction skills and technical depth in areas like RAG, MCP, and agent architecture.

![AI Super Agent Project](https://pic.yupi.icu/1/image-20260302181221444.png)

AI Hotspot Monitoring Tool: focuses on AI coding ability and AI’s practical application in the real world, showing that you can use a Vibe Coding approach to quickly and independently deliver a truly useful tool product from requirement analysis to solution design to development and launch.

![AI Hotspot Monitoring Tool](https://pic.yupi.icu/1285/202603021602038.png)

The benefit of this pairing is that interviewers can see both your solid traditional technical foundation and your new reserve of AI application-development knowledge and hands-on AI coding experience.

#### AI Super Agent

There are several things about how this project is written that are worth learning from.

![](https://pic.yupi.icu/1/image-20260330163946480.png)

1) You must launch your project online, and include an accessible URL and GitHub. In an interviewer’s eyes, "can run locally" and "can be deployed online for users" are two totally different levels.

2) Try to include quantified data in every bullet. For example: API call costs reduced by 60%, knowledge Q&A accuracy improved by 45%, user waiting time reduced by 80%. Both are describing optimization, but "optimized performance" and "improved performance by 45%" feel completely different. The latter is far more convincing. Of course, don’t fabricate numbers. It’s best if they come from your real testing.

3) Make the technical details more specific. For example, "Passed user identity information through ToolContext and combined parameter validation to prevent invalid execution caused by tool-calling hallucinations." That one sentence includes the technical solution, the problem solved, and the result. It feels very concrete and credible.

#### AI Hotspot Monitoring Tool

![](https://pic.yupi.icu/1/image-20260330164503926.png)

The biggest highlight of this project is the first bullet:

> More than 90% of the code in the project was generated by AI, greatly improving development efficiency

Some students may hesitate: if I write "the code was generated by AI," won’t that hurt me? Quite the opposite! In 2026, efficiently using AI coding tools is itself a capability. The key is not who wrote the code. The key is whether you can control the overall process — requirement analysis, solution design, technology selection, testing, validation, deployment. You are leading those steps. AI is only helping you accelerate each one.

Another highlight is the packaging of Agent Skills, which shows that you’re not just a user of AI tools — you can also contribute new capabilities to them. Most people use AI coding tools only to help themselves write code, but you can also develop Skills that make AI coding tools stronger. Having that awareness alone is enough to give your job hunt an extra edge.

#### Other Personal Projects

For students with less than 3 years of work experience, it’s enough to focus on 1 to 3 projects on the resume. Quality matters more than quantity. But if you’ve done more projects, you can add an "Other Personal Projects" section where each project gets one line that highlights the tech stack and the core selling point.

![](https://pic.yupi.icu/1/image-20260330164758514.png)

Notice the guiding sentence at the beginning of this section, which points interviewers to the personal portfolio website. If they’re interested, they can go there directly and see more project details. In the limited space of a resume, that lets you pack in far more information than a few pages of paper ever could.

I believe the most ideal resume project combination right now is: an AI application development project + an AI coding project + a technical wheel / infrastructure-style project. That way, you show mastery of new AI technologies, demonstrate understanding of lower-level principles, and also prove that you can use AI to quickly deliver products.



### Personal Strengths

The core principle of this final section is: **show both your professionalism and the breadth of your abilities, and provide evidence for every single point.**

![](https://pic.yupi.icu/1/image-20260330165412460.png)

Many students write personal strengths like this: I learn quickly, I’m good at teamwork, I love programming... But who can’t write that? Interviewers gain basically nothing from reading it.

But once you add evidence, it becomes completely different:

- Say that you have "strong full-stack ability," and then back it up with "launched 10+ accessible products," with real products people can actually open
- Say that you "love open source," and then back it up with "20k+ followers, 10k stars" — the data speaks for itself
- Say that you have "strong learning ability," and then back it up with "can independently read official documentation" — that is a concrete behavior

This section is not mandatory. If you really can’t find convincing examples to support it, then you’re better off deleting the section entirely and using the space for project experience instead.



## Final Words

Let’s review it again. To write a strong programmer resume for the AI era, the core is doing these 3 things well:

1) **Show your pursuit of new technologies**: AI application development, AI coding tools, and AI coding paradigms are not things you deliberately write just to impress interviewers. They should be things you’re genuinely using and genuinely learning. A programmer who stays curious about new technologies will always grow much faster than someone who stands still.

2) **Show your independence**: the thing interviewers fear most is reading "participated in xx project" and then nothing more. They need to confirm that you are someone who can independently take ownership — independently build projects, launch them, operate them, and solve problems, instead of only mechanically completing tasks.

3) **Match company needs in both directions**: every skill and every project you write should be aligned with the company’s JD. For example, if the hiring description says they need someone who can do AI application development, then you should have AI-related projects. If they need someone who understands monitoring and operations, then you should have observability-building experience.

I recommend preparing your resume as early as possible. Don’t wait until right before job hunting to start. Use the resume as a goal, and work backward to guide your learning direction. Every time you finish learning a technology or completing a project, come back and update your resume. You’ll find that the blank spaces on the page keep shrinking, and your confidence keeps growing.
