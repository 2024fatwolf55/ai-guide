## What Is GEO?

Before GEO appeared, you may have already heard of SEO.

SEO stands for Search Engine Optimization. Its goal is to help your website get indexed by search engines like Baidu and Google and rank higher in search results, thereby gaining more clicks and traffic.

GEO stands for Generative Engine Optimization. You can think of it as SEO for the AI era. Its goal is no longer to compete for search rankings, but to get your content cited and recommended by large AI models such as ChatGPT, DeepSeek, and Doubao, so that your content becomes an information source in AI-generated answers.

To use an analogy, SEO is like fighting for a golden spot on a supermarket shelf so customers can see your product at a glance; GEO is like getting the store clerk to actively recommend your product when a customer asks for advice.

![](https://pic.yupi.icu/1/image-20260327163145093.png)

And the traffic quality from GEO may even be higher than SEO. Some data shows that traffic from AI search can convert at **more than 5 times** the rate of traditional search!

The reason isn’t hard to understand. When users ask AI, their needs are often more explicit, and AI recommendations are more targeted. That’s why people increasingly prefer asking AI over using a traditional search engine.

To be clear, there is nothing wrong with GEO technology itself. Just like SEO, it’s simply a form of content optimization. What’s truly problematic is using false information to “poison” AI. That’s similar to the “black-hat SEO” of the old days, where people deceived search engines with keyword stuffing, hidden text, and spam backlinks. Search engines spent years fighting black-hat SEO, and I believe AI model providers will also strengthen detection and defense against GEO poisoning as the technology evolves.

Think about it carefully: how many people today are mass-producing huge amounts of AI-generated content and publishing it across every platform just to capture traffic? Even if the content isn’t fake, it’s often bland, repetitive, and nutritionally empty. Isn’t that also another form of “poisoning” the information environment of the internet?

Legitimate GEO is not about polluting AI. It’s about helping your high-quality content get discovered and cited by AI more effectively.

So how do you do it?

## How to Do GEO Well

I’ve summarized 8 practical GEO optimization tactics. For each one, I’ll explain it by analogy using the idea of “treat AI like a person,” so you’ll not only know what to do, but also understand why it works.

### 1. Give the Conclusion First

When AI extracts content, it especially favors articles that **provide the core answer right at the beginning**.

Why?

Imagine AI as an intern who has to read millions of articles every day. Its job is to find content that can answer user questions.

If your article starts with empty filler like “As everyone knows, with the development of the times...”, that intern will immediately flip to the next article.

So the correct approach is: **answer the core question directly at the beginning of the article, give the conclusion first, and then explain it in sections afterward.**

For example, my [interview practice website](https://www.mianshiya.com/) contains many interview questions. If you treat each question like an article, then the question itself is the title, and the explanation is the body. Every explanation first gives the key answer, and only then moves into detailed explanation and extended knowledge.

Content like this is not only loved by AI, but also comfortable for people to read.

![](https://pic.yupi.icu/1/image-20260327112948642.png)



### 2. Write in a Structured Way

When AI tries to understand article content, it particularly likes content that is **clearly formatted and hierarchically organized**.

Think about it this way: during an interview, the interviewer receives two resumes. One is neatly formatted and divided into sections such as work experience, project experience, and professional skills. The other is just one giant paragraph of self-introduction. Which one do you think the interviewer would rather read?

AI follows the same logic. Structured content is not only easier for AI to parse and extract, but it also tends to receive more citation weight than plain text.

Recommended structured formats:

1) Q&A format

Q: What is GEO?

A: GEO refers to...

2) Step-by-step lists: step one... step two... step three...

3) Comparison tables

4) Hierarchical headings: H1 > H2 > H3 nested in order. I recommend writing articles in Markdown.

For example, the explanations on Mianshiya use hierarchical headings based on knowledge points, which also makes readers’ thinking much clearer while reading.

![](https://pic.yupi.icu/1/image-20260327145959216.png)



### 3. Create Professional and Authoritative Content

AI is like people: it only dares to cite things that have evidence behind them.

Think about what your advisor says most often when you write a paper:

“Where is the data source? Where are the references?”

When AI decides which content to cite, it performs a similar kind of verification. If your article contains concrete numbers, clearly labeled data sources, and citations from authoritative institutions, AI is more inclined to trust and cite it.

AI also has a clear preference for **professional depth** in content. It’s like when you’re sick and search online—would you trust a professional science-style article written by a doctor, or a random forum user sharing personal experience?

AI judges in a similar way. Some studies show that more than about 80% of sources cited by AI contain at least three signals of authority, such as professional credentials, institutional endorsement, or verifiable data.

So when writing content, pay attention to these points:

- Use specific data instead of vague descriptions. “Increased by 45%” is far better than “grew quickly,” and be sure to cite the source, for example “according to XX’s XX annual report”
- Cite research, academic papers, or official reports from authoritative institutions to support your viewpoint
- Include real cases and test results to demonstrate the author’s professional background and practical experience, such as “after our team tested 30 tools, we found...”
- Sign the article and associate it with a real author bio, so AI can identify “who wrote this”



### 4. Directly Answer the Questions Users Actually Ask

The essence of AI search is helping users find answers. What users say to AI is usually phrased as questions, such as: what is XX? How do I do XX? Which is better, XX or YY?

You can imagine AI as a student frantically looking for answers before an exam. The test paper is full of questions, and the student needs to find the best-matching answers from the reference materials. If your article happens to be built around those exact questions and provides direct, clear answers, then your article becomes that student’s favorite reference book.

Specifically, you can do this:

- First research which questions users in your field most often ask AI
- Organize the article directly in a Q&A format
- Use question-style titles whenever possible, for example “How do you do GEO well?” is more user-friendly than “Some GEO methods”
- Let each article focus on answering 1 to 3 core questions. Don’t try to swallow too much at once

For example, many questions on my Mianshiya platform have an “interview Q&A” function that predicts what interviewers may ask. This not only helps users prepare better for interviews, but also benefits GEO.

![](https://pic.yupi.icu/1/1773743844725-d9a20d72-bec2-44be-bb0b-486def4cd68e.png)



### 5. Publish More on Authoritative Platforms and Build Reputation

When AI decides whom to cite, one very important factor is **how many trustworthy sources have mentioned you**.

It’s similar to job hunting. If your resume contains only self-evaluation like “I am highly capable,” the recruiter will probably laugh. But if you have recommendation letters from former leaders at major companies, contribution records in well-known open-source projects, or even speaking experience at industry conferences, recruiters will take you seriously.

If your product has been recommended by big Zhihu creators, covered by industry media, or cited by authoritative websites, AI’s trust score for you will naturally be higher.

And when AI crawlers scan internet content, they don’t treat all platforms equally. They pay more attention to platforms with stronger authority and higher content quality. So you should prioritize publishing on such platforms, and do it across multiple platforms:

- Zhihu, WeChat Official Accounts (high-authority domestic platforms)
- GitHub, Stack Overflow (technical platforms)
- Xiaohongshu, Bilibili (younger user communities)
- Vertical industry media and forums
- Your brand’s official website (AI crawlers attach more importance to official-site content)

Another small trick: some AI platforms may prioritize citing content from within their own ecosystems. For example, videos you publish on Douyin may be more easily cited by Doubao; articles published on WeChat Official Accounts may be more easily crawled by Tencent Yuanbao. So if you want to optimize specifically for a certain AI platform, you can prioritize publishing on its related ecosystem platforms.



### 6. Do Multimodal Content Well

AI doesn’t only look at text. Today’s mainstream AI search engines can already index images, videos, and other content formats.

It’s like shopping online: reading text-only product descriptions feels very different from seeing real product photos plus video explanations. AI works in a similar way. The richer and more diverse your content is, the higher AI’s trust in it tends to be.

So when publishing content, remember to write good Alt descriptions for images instead of leaving them blank. For videos, it’s best to provide subtitles and timestamped chapters.

Also, for tables and charts, try to use HTML or Markdown tables rather than screenshots, so AI can extract the data directly. If you explain knowledge using images, provide a plain-text explanation alongside them.



### 7. Keep Content Fresh

AI is like people: it likes the new and gets tired of the old.

When AI faces multiple information sources, it is more inclined to cite **newer content with clear time markers**. The reason is simple: if a user asks, “What are the best AI coding tools in 2026?”, AI is obviously not going to cite an article written in 2023.

So I recommend building a few good habits:

- Mark time information on articles, such as “updated in X month, 20XX”
- Update the data and cases in core content once per quarter
- Revise or label outdated content in a timely way
- Keep publishing new content instead of relying only on a few old articles forever

### 8. Optimize the Technical Side

This one is more technical, but it’s very important if you’re building products or websites.

You can think of an AI crawler like a guest visiting your home. If your front door is locked (`robots.txt` blocks AI crawlers), the house is a mess (no structured data), and the lights are off (the page depends entirely on JS rendering so the crawler only sees a blank page), then the guest will turn around and leave, let alone say nice things about you.

Specifically, pay attention to these technical details:

- Make sure `robots.txt` allows AI crawlers to access your site (`GPTBot`, `ClaudeBot`, `PerplexityBot`, etc.)
- Add Schema structured markup, using JSON-LD to label things like FAQ, Product, and Organization, so AI can understand your content more easily
- Use SSR or SSG to ensure AI crawlers receive complete HTML pages rather than just a pile of JS scripts
- Use `https`, which is now a basic trust threshold for websites
- Submit an XML Sitemap to help AI crawlers discover your content faster

You’ll notice that these optimization methods are actually closely connected to traditional SEO technical optimization. In fact, doing SEO well also helps GEO, and the two do not conflict.



## How Do You Verify GEO Results?

After doing GEO optimization, how do you know whether it worked?

The method is actually simple: take your most important business questions and ask them to mainstream AI tools like ChatGPT, DeepSeek, Doubao, and Perplexity. Then see whether their answers mention your product or content. I recommend asking the same questions once a week and recording the trend over time.

Also, don’t test with only one wording. Real users ask questions in many different ways, so testing from multiple angles gives a more accurate picture.

If asking manually feels too troublesome, you can also have AI help you write an automated monitoring script that regularly submits questions to major AI platforms and records whether the answers contain your target keywords.

There are already some ready-made GEO monitoring tools on the market that can automatically track how often your brand is cited in answers across major AI platforms, so you can look into those if you’re interested.

![](https://pic.yupi.icu/1/image-20260327162329991.png)

One thing to note is that AI answers are inherently uncertain. If you ask the same question twice, you may get different answers. So don’t panic just because you weren’t cited once. GEO is about long-term trends, not one-off results.
